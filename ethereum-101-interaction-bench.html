First round

Four benches, built to the WorldComputer bar

These are the four cards with the most to gain and no interactive yet. Each one is a model with real state that survives being played with, not a question with an animation bolted on. Motion is frame-driven with hand-picked easing, colours come from theme.css, and every gesture teaches the specific claim the card makes.

Chapter 6 · making things happen · the-blockchain

Build it, then break it

Teaches why history is tamper-evident: each block's seal is computed from the one before it.

pending2 tx

Why this beats a quiz. The hashes are really computed from the block contents, so a learner who edits a transaction sees a specific seal change to a specific new value — and then watches the break travel forward one block at a time. Repairing it costs them every block after the edit, which is the whole security argument, felt rather than asserted.

Chapter 2 · more than money · smart-contracts

The machine doesn't decide — the code did

Teaches deterministic execution and all-or-nothing revert: a failed condition undoes everything, including the payment.

contract holds0.00 ETH
// Snack.sol — anyone can read this
function buy() external payable {
·  require(msg.value >= 0.05 ether);
·  require(stock > 0);
·  require(block.timestamp < closesAt);
  stock -= 1;
  _dispense(msg.sender);
}
machine state — stock3

Why this beats a quiz. The learner sets up the failure themselves — underpay, empty the shelf, or close the window — and the source they can read predicts exactly which line stops them. Watching the coin come back out of the return tray while stock stays put is the clearest possible picture of a revert.

Second round

Five more, and five different verbs

Ten of the eighteen cards end their copy with [Interactive/image/game to be added], so the appetite is already written into the lab. The trap is giving them all the same gesture; sameness is what makes a set of interactions feel thin. Each bench below is a different kind of doing: sorting, searching, noticing, scrubbing, decoding.

Chapter 4 · your money, your keys · wallets · sorting

What's actually inside the wallet?

Teaches the card's headline correction: an Ethereum wallet holds your key, not your money.

sorted0 of 6
on your device0
on the ethereum network0

Drag a card, or tap it and then tap a column.

Why sorting fits here. The misconception is spatial — people picture ETH sitting inside an app like cash in a billfold. Making them physically place "your ETH" on the network side, and get told why when they put it on the phone, corrects the mental picture in the same shape the picture is wrong in. A wrong drop is refused with a reason rather than just scored.

Chapter 3 · why not just use a bank? · the-system-we-grew-up-with · searching

Go on, read the terms

Teaches the card's actual claim — that checking the rules was never really an option — by making the learner try.

Find the clause that lets them close your account without telling you why. 0.0s
Northgate Savings — account terms · illustrative example, not a real bank
The same power, written as a smart contract

// Vault.sol — the complete rules. All of them.

function deposit() external payable { balance[msg.sender] += msg.value; }

function withdraw(uint amt) external { balance[msg.sender] -= amt; pay(msg.sender, amt); }

function transfer(address to, uint amt) external { ... }

// there is no freeze function, and no owner who could add one

Find the clause that lets them close your account.

Why searching fits here. The card says nobody reads the rules and you proceed on faith. Told that, a learner nods. Made to hunt for one clause in a wall of six-point serif while a timer runs, they feel it — and then the same question against four lines of readable code answers itself in a second. The contrast is the argument, and giving up is a legitimate ending.

Chapter 3 · why not just use a bank? · rules-can-change-on-you · noticing

You agreed to this. Apparently.

Teaches the difference between a rule that changes quietly overnight and one that can only change in the open.

Northgate Savings — agreement v4.2

Why noticing fits here. No question is asked and no answer is scored. The learner reads four clauses, clicks "go to sleep", and finds two of them quietly rewritten in the morning with a version number they never approved. Discovering a change you were never told about is the entire point of the card, so discovery is the mechanic.

Chapter 4 · your money, your keys · ether · scrubbing

You don't need a whole one

Teaches the card's most practically useful line: ETH splits into very small fractions, so you can start with any amount.

ether1
gwei1,000,000,000
wei1,000,000,000,000,000,000
1 wei1 gwei1 ETH

if ETH were $3,000, then buys 0.016666 ETH
Why scrubbing fits here. "Divisible to eighteen decimal places" is a sentence people skim. A slider they drag from one ETH down to one wei, with all three units counting in step, turns the abstraction into a distance they travelled with their thumb. The dollar row exists because "do I need to buy a whole one?" is the actual question a beginner is holding, and the numbers are exact rather than floating-point approximations.

Chapter 6 · making things happen · transactions · decoding

The label is not the transaction

Teaches both of the card's claims at once: a transaction is one signed instruction, and what that instruction does lives in a field most people never look at.

read before signing0 of 4
the transactionnothing loaded

Why decoding fits here. Every other bench teaches a fact; this one teaches a habit, and it is the habit that decides whether someone keeps their money. The four errands are a deliberate ladder — no data at all, then no data but the recipient is a program, then data that moves value while value still reads 0, then data that hands over everything. The calldata is really ABI-encoded against genuine selectors (0xa9059cbb, 0xa22cb465), so a learner who later pastes real hex into a decoder meets the same anatomy. It also finishes a thought WalletSafety only starts: that card says reject anything unclear, and this one shows you how to make it clear.
